# For Dad

### Remarks for the Celebration of Life — September 19, 2026

---

Before I tell you about my dad, I want to start with Mom.

Mom and Dad met at the University of Oklahoma. They shared sixty-six years of marriage, raised Franci, Alan, and me, and became grandparents to Weston, Kendall, Marlis, Berit, and Ian.

When I talk about my childhood—the moves, the trips, the places we called home—those are memories of the life they built together.

Mom, thank you for that life.

One of the gifts Dad gave me was a curiosity about the world.

---

When I was in second grade, we lived in Amman, Jordan. We drove from Texas through Mexico to Acapulco—for Christmas, as a family, in a car. He took me to Greece. When he was at UT, we spent our summers camping on a piece of land he'd bought outside Nederland, Colorado, and I am fairly sure the point of that land was never the land. It was the drive, and the stories we told about the drive for the next forty years.

When Dad took the job at the University of Washington, we packed up and set off from Austin for Seattle in a little caravan: a car pulling a boat, my brother's truck and camper, and a dog and cat along for the ride. We made that long drive an adventure, traveling through the national parks along the way. We'd pull into campgrounds after dark and wake up in the morning to mountainsides towering above us.

And those car trips had music. Dad would sing songs from his fraternity days at OU, and Tom Lehrer songs like “Be Prepared,” “The Hunting Song,” and “So Long, Mom.” His collection covered a lot of ground.

There were the Mills Brothers and the Kingston Trio—“Greenback Dollar,” “Tom Dooley,” “MTA.” And there were Willie Nelson and Jerry Jeff Walker. As a kid, I learned *Viva Terlingua!* and *Wanted! The Outlaws* by heart.

He was a good piano player, too, with a small but varied repertoire. He'd sit down and play while the rest of us were getting ready to go somewhere. When he got to the boogie-woogie, you knew it was time to go.

Those songs are part of how I remember Dad. I hear them, and I can hear him singing in the car or playing the piano while we got ready. He gave me places I wanted to go back to, and music that still takes me there.

Some of that education happened close to home. Growing up in Austin, I got to know professors from the University of Texas and go to faculty outings in the parks around town. Through Dad, I met people like Barbara Jordan, Walt Rostow, and Elizabeth Rostow. I got to talk with them and learn about their lives and the history they'd been part of.

Those conversations helped give me a love of knowledge and a desire to understand. Dad opened that world to me while I was still a kid.

He had a PhD in economics from Rice. He spent his life studying how things work. But the lesson of his that I carry every day reached far beyond a classroom:

**The world is one of the best educations you can get. Go see it.**

He gave me wanderlust. I've spent thirty years trying to hand it to my kids.

---

Here is the thing I've come to understand as I've listened to our family remember him.

He didn't just encourage us to see the world. He encouraged us to make up our own minds about it.

Ask my daughter Marlis. During the BLM marches, her grandfather asked her what she thought. Not to correct her—to hear it. The debate that followed got loud enough that her mother had to shut it down, because the two of them had emptied the lake house. Everyone else left. They kept going.

That is a love language. Not everyone recognizes it, but our family does.

When Marlis was thirteen he asked her where in the world she would go if she could go anywhere. She said Greece.

That was not a rhetorical question. That November, we all went to Athens.

Marlis spent almost every lunch of her junior year at the Conservatory eating with her grandparents. I love that detail. Athens was extraordinary. But so was having a place at their table, day after day.

He told her he was proud she was a teacher—although he also thought she would make an amazing lawyer. One of the last things he said to her was, “I am happy you found something you love doing.”

Ask my son Ian. Grandpa wanted to know about everything—rugby, his politics, the clubs he was in. They disagreed often. And Ian says that's what made him proud to hold a position and defend it. In the fall of 2020, when the pandemic had left him with just one planned college visit, my father drove him to Rapid City. Then he took his grandson to a rooftop bar in South Dakota and they had oysters.

Ian says he will carry that confidence with him: his grandfather believed he could do it. That is something you can leave a grandchild that lasts a lifetime.

Ask Berit. Chess lessons, terrible jokes, arguments for sport. A week on a cruise ship with Grandpa, Grandma, and Aunt Franci in 2019. And the last conversation they had: good luck with your show, I'm proud of you, and I hope the Rangers make the playoffs.

That's the whole man in one sentence.

---

Lesley helped me put something else into words.

Jared worked to understand her. He supported us every time we moved our family across creation, and he never once asked why. He just showed up. He visited every house we ever lived in—including Łódź, Poland.

Lesley remembered how proud he was of the family he and Mom created. Franci, Alan, and I are different people, and he gave each of us the support he thought we needed to succeed. With his grandchildren, he listened, challenged them, and loved them as they became themselves.

He was a pillar of this family. He did not tell us he loved us nearly as often as he showed us.

---

Years ago, when his friend George Douglas died, Dad and Wade Clifton opened a bottle of twelve-year Balvenie DoubleWood and drank to him.

In Portugal, our friends helped us raise a glass to Dad—with Manhattans made with Gentleman Jack and port. I think he'd have had notes on the recipe.

---

Dad—

You taught me to be curious.

To have an opinion, and a reason for it.

To go see what was around the next corner.

And I promise I'll keep going.

And your grandchildren will, too.

Marlis, Ian, and Berit are the best of what you taught me, and I see you in them every single day.

I love you.

Safe travels.
