# Jared Earl Hazleton

## Economist, Educator, Public-Policy Scholar, Business Leader, and Institutional Builder

### A researched biographical profile, 1937–2026

### Research note

This profile draws on University of Texas System records, University of North Texas archives and catalogs, Federal Reserve archival material, scholarly bibliographic databases, University of Texas Press, ERIC, the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation, contemporary Texas newspapers, TexEcon, and the family's published obituary. Where a later biography conflicts with a contemporary institutional record, I have generally given greater weight to the contemporary or primary institutional source.

One issue remains unresolved enough that it should **not yet be stated as settled fact**: the exact year Jared received his Ph.D. from Rice University. TexEcon gives **1961**, while other family material has used later dates. His dissertation-derived book says unequivocally that the study began as a Rice doctoral dissertation supported by a Resources for the Future fellowship, and TexEcon records the John W. Gardner Award in **1965**, but I have not yet located a Rice commencement or registrar record that conclusively establishes the degree-conferral year. ([TexEcon][1])

---

## A life spent moving between ideas and their consequences

Dr. Jared Earl Hazleton's professional life is difficult to capture with a single occupational label. He was an economist, but he was also a university professor and dean, a Federal Reserve researcher, an international-development specialist, a public-policy analyst, an energy-industry executive, a consultant, and a civic leader.

The consistency in that seemingly varied career was an interest in how economic ideas worked when they encountered actual institutions, resources, governments, industries, and communities.

His subjects changed remarkably over six decades. At different points he worked on commercial banking, fisheries, sulphur and mineral resources, environmental land-use policy, Middle Eastern development, land reform, public finance, presidential macroeconomic management, Texas taxation, education finance, the oil and gas industry, regional economic development, school calendars, NAFTA, housing, title insurance, and the economic consequences of climate change. His bibliography is unusually broad because his career repeatedly crossed the boundary between academic economics and applied decision-making. ([Google Books][2])

That boundary-crossing quality was recognized when the University of North Texas recruited him as dean in 1999. UNT Chancellor Alfred Hurley described the breadth of his background in academia, government, and business, while the university noted that his experience included the Federal Reserve, university administration, international development, Texas public policy, the energy industry, and economic research. ([UNT Digital Library][3])

# Early life and the University of Oklahoma

Jared Earl Hazleton was born September 12, 1937, in Oklahoma City, Oklahoma. TexEcon identifies his parents as Alfred Larson Hazleton and Myrtle Frances Hazleton; the funeral-home notice uses the spelling “Myrtle Francis,” a small discrepancy that family records could settle definitively. ([TexEcon][1])

He attended the University of Oklahoma and graduated in **1959**. The family obituary identifies his degree as a **Bachelor of Business Administration**, while UNT's 1999 institutional biography describes it as a bachelor's degree **in accounting**. A later UNT catalog abbreviates it as a **BS**, which appears inconsistent with the other records. The most defensible formulation at present is therefore:

**B.B.A., University of Oklahoma, 1959, with studies in accounting.** ([Legacy.com][4])

His published obituary records a notably active undergraduate career. He participated in ROTC, belonged to Beta Theta Pi and Beta Gamma Sigma, and served as vice president of the Student Senate. Most consequentially for his personal life, OU was where he met Elaine, who became his wife of 66 years. ([Legacy.com][4])

This period already hints at several themes that would continue throughout his life: leadership, public service, economics and business, and the partnership with Elaine that accompanied the rest of his career.

---

# Naval service

After graduating from Oklahoma, Jared served as a U.S. Navy officer. TexEcon gives his service as **1959–1961** and identifies him as a lieutenant in the Supply Corps, U.S. Naval Reserve/U.S. Navy. The family obituary adds an important specific detail: he served aboard the destroyer escort **USS *Howard D. Crow***. ([TexEcon][1])

I have not yet located a digitized Navy personnel roster independently tying Jared to the *Howard D. Crow*, so the ship assignment currently rests on family documentation rather than an independently located Navy archive. The broader Navy service and approximate 1959–61 period, however, are consistent across the biographical record.

His Supply Corps service is also an interesting precursor to his later professional interests. The Supply Corps concerns logistics, finance, procurement, and resource allocation—practical questions not far removed from the kinds of economic problems that became the center of his professional life.

# Rice University and the beginnings of an economist

After his Naval service, Jared pursued doctoral study in economics at Rice University.

His doctoral research focused on the sulphur industry. The work eventually became *The Economics of the Sulphur Industry*, published in 1970 for Resources for the Future. In the book's acknowledgments, Jared explicitly states that the project began as a **doctoral dissertation prepared for Rice University under a Natural Resources Fellowship awarded by Resources for the Future**. He credits Henry Steele as director of his thesis committee and Gaston Rimlinger and Dwight Brothers as readers, while also acknowledging Harvard economist Edward S. Mason for suggesting sulphur as a research topic. ([Amazon S3][5])

The resulting study was much more than a narrow commodity history. Jared examined market structure, costs, barriers to entry, supply, demand, oligopolistic behavior, resource scarcity, alternative sources of sulphur, and the public-welfare implications of industry behavior. He also considered sulphur recovered through pollution-control processes—an intersection of resource economics and environmental policy that would remain relevant long after the original publication. ([Taylor & Francis][6])

Resources for the Future later described the book as one of its resource-appraisal studies and identified Jared, by 1970, as a lecturer in economics at the University of Texas at Austin. ([Amazon S3][5])

There is also a revealingly personal note in the acknowledgments. After thanking academic mentors, researchers, industry participants, and Resources for the Future, Jared closes by thanking his wife and family, joking that they “also serve who only stand and wait and wait and wait.” The book was dedicated to them. ([Amazon S3][5])

That brief line gives the scholarly record something a career chronology cannot: evidence of the humor and family awareness behind the professional work.

### The unresolved Ph.D. date

TexEcon currently lists the Ph.D. as **1961** and separately lists the **John W. Gardner Award, Rice University, 1965**. ([TexEcon][1])

Because Jared was already working for the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston by 1964, it is entirely possible that coursework, dissertation completion, award recognition, employment, and publication occurred on different dates. The book itself does not state the degree year. Until a Rice commencement program, transcript, diploma, dissertation catalog entry, or registrar record is obtained, I would write simply:

**“He earned a Ph.D. in Economics from Rice University.”**

That is firmly established; the year is not yet sufficiently established for a permanent memorial record.

# Federal Reserve Bank of Boston, 1964–1968

Jared's early professional career placed him at the intersection of academic economics and the banking system.

TexEcon records him as a **Research Economist at the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston, 1964–1965; manager in the research department, 1965–1966; and banking-services officer, 1966–1968**. ([TexEcon][1])

Independent Federal Reserve archival material confirms his Boston Fed affiliation in a particularly interesting context. In the late 1960s, bank credit cards and check-credit plans were sufficiently new that the Federal Reserve System created a task group to study them. The group surveyed banks, examined plans operated by oil companies, department stores, and nonbank card companies, and helped revise Federal Reserve consumer-credit data collection. Jared Hazleton was the **Boston Federal Reserve representative** on that task group. ([FRASER][7])

It is a small but fascinating historical detail: early in his career Jared was helping the Federal Reserve understand a financial instrument that would later become an ordinary feature of American life.

His work in Boston also led into fisheries economics. Along with economist Frederick W. Bell, he edited *Recent Developments and Research in Fisheries Economics*, published in 1967. The 233-page volume grew from a conference on New England fisheries economics sponsored in part by the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston and the New England Economic Research Foundation. ([Google Books][2])

The fisheries work demonstrates an emerging pattern: Jared was willing to apply economic analysis to a concrete regional industry where questions of natural resources, capital investment, technology, regulation, and community livelihoods converged.

---

# The University of Texas at Austin, 1968–1980

The transition from the Federal Reserve to academia can be documented unusually precisely.

A University of Texas Board of Regents docket from July 1968 records **Jared E. Hazleton as a Research Scientist in the Center for Economic Development with academic status as Lecturer in Economics**. A November 1968 docket records his economics appointment for the 1968–69 academic year. ([The University of Texas System][8])

By 1971 he held a joint role involving economics and the newly established Lyndon B. Johnson School of Public Affairs. UT records show him as a lecturer associated with the LBJ School and Economics, and by early 1973 he was explicitly identified as an **Associate Professor in the LBJ School**. ([The University of Texas System][9])

The university's later biographical account adds that he served as **vice chair of the UT economics department**, joined the LBJ School faculty, and ultimately became an **associate dean** there. ([UNT Digital Library][3])

By 1980 UT records identify him as **Professor and Associate Dean, Lyndon B. Johnson School of Public Affairs**. ([The University of Texas System][10])

The progression is therefore well supported:

**1968:** Lecturer in Economics / Research Scientist
**early 1970s:** LBJ School faculty, Associate Professor
**later 1970s:** Professor and Associate Dean
**1980:** still documented as Professor and Associate Dean immediately before moving to Washington

This was an intellectually productive period.

# Natural resources, environment, and public policy

Jared's scholarly work at UT moved well beyond his original sulphur-industry research.

In 1972 he published **“The Outlook for Sulfur”** in the *Texas Business Review*. The article was adapted from work presented at a forum on energy resources and materials policy. A UT annual report also identifies his contribution **“Sulfur Resource Policy”** among papers addressing energy, mineral resources, pollution, nuclear power, and national materials policy. ([EconBiz][11])

He also participated in interdisciplinary environmental research. With Abraham Charnes, Kingsley Haynes, and Michael Ryan, Jared coauthored **“A Hierarchical Goal-Programming Approach to Environmental Land Use Management.”** The research developed a mathematical framework for evaluating competing environmental land-use goals across statewide, multicounty, and local scales on the Texas Gulf Coast. ([Cantaloupe Image Server][12])

The combination is characteristic of his career: industrial economics, resources, quantitative modeling, and public-policy consequences rather than economics isolated from institutional choices.

---

# The Ford Foundation and the Middle East, 1973–1975

From **1973 to 1975**, Jared took leave from the University of Texas to work as an **economic research specialist for the Ford Foundation**, assigned to the **Royal Scientific Society in Amman, Jordan**. UNT's 1999 biography explicitly documents the assignment and dates. ([UNT Digital Library][3])

UT's own 1973 records provide contemporary corroboration: his summer appointment moved between the Center for Research in Water Resources and the LBJ School using Ford Foundation contract funds. ([The University of Texas System][13])

His Middle Eastern research was substantive rather than ceremonial.

In 1974, the Royal Scientific Society's Economic Research Department published his study **“The Impact of the East Ghor Canal Project on Land Consolidation, Distribution and Tenure.”** The work examined land reform and the economic effects of irrigation development in the Jordan Valley. Later scholarship on Jordanian irrigation continued to cite the study. ([EconBiz][14])

That research became the basis for the later article **“Land Reform in Jordan: The East Ghor Canal Project,”** published in *Middle Eastern Studies* in 1979. It examined land redistribution, tenure, agricultural output, and living standards associated with the irrigation project. ([JSTOR][15])

His work also extended to monetary and fiscal issues and Persian Gulf development. Bibliographic records identify his 1974 Royal Scientific Society work on monetary analysis and his later research on the challenges posed by rapidly increasing petroleum revenues. ([Amazon S3][16])

The latter became **“Gold Rush Economics: Development Planning in the Persian/Arabian Gulf.”** An LBJ School publication list shows an earlier 1976 version, while *Studies in Comparative International Development* published the journal article in 1978. Jared examined the unusual development problems facing oil-exporting states after the dramatic 1973 increase in petroleum prices. ([CiteSeerX][17])

These years significantly broadened his intellectual territory. His economics now encompassed development planning, petroleum wealth, land reform, monetary policy, irrigation, and public finance in emerging economies.

---

# The LBJ School and public-affairs leadership

After returning from Jordan, Jared continued to rise at the LBJ School.

By the late 1970s he was not simply teaching economics; he was helping administer a professional school devoted to applying scholarship to government and public affairs. A 1979 international conference program identifies him as **Associate Dean of the Lyndon Baines Johnson School of Public Affairs**. ([Digital Collections][18])

UT records also show him supervising international public-affairs work, including a 1979 participant-training program and a 1980 USAID internship in Lima, Peru. ([The University of Texas System][19])

This stage helps explain the rest of his career. He was moving increasingly toward roles in which economic analysis, institutional management, and public decisions were intertwined.

---

# Dean at the University of Washington, 1980–1982

In **1980**, Jared left Texas to become dean of the **Graduate School of Public Affairs at the University of Washington**.

TexEcon gives the period as 1980–1982, and multiple independent sources corroborate the appointment. A 1982 Alfred P. Sloan Foundation report identifies **Jared Hazleton, Dean of the Graduate School of Public Affairs**, as project director for a University of Washington program designed to strengthen students' preparation in economics, applied mathematics, and communications for public-policy graduate study. ([TexEcon][1])

Later institutional references continued to identify him as the former Washington dean. ([Angelo State University][20])

His stay in Seattle was relatively brief, but the deanship represented a major step in a career increasingly focused on connecting analytical training with public leadership.

---

# President of the Texas Research League, 1982–1987

Jared returned to Texas in **1982** to become president of the **Texas Research League**, an Austin-based, privately supported nonprofit that studied state and local government and public policy. TexEcon dates his tenure from 1982 to 1987. Contemporary sources repeatedly identify him as the League's president in the middle of that period. ([TexEcon][1])

This was one of the most publicly visible phases of his career.

The League analyzed taxation, state finance, local government, education spending, and Texas's changing economy. In 1984, for example, Jared discussed a League study examining how Texas school districts allocated instructional spending. ([Texas Tech University Newspapers][21])

His **May 9, 1985 keynote address**, “New Directions for Texas,” examined structural change in the Texas economy and the state's dependence on oil prices. The surviving League publication emphasizes his concern with how major economic shifts would affect businesses, governments, and families over the longer term. ([The Portal to Texas History][22])

During Texas's fiscal and energy downturn in 1986, he was frequently quoted explaining the relationship between state budget actions and local government finances. In one Associated Press report, he pointed out that reductions in state responsibilities could shift costs to cities, counties, and local school districts. ([Southwest Special Collections][23])

The role also made him a participant in broader discussions about education. He appeared with state education officials in a 1986 leadership program for Texas school administrators. ([Southwest Special Collections][24])

Most valuable for historians and family members is a surviving **30-minute television interview recorded April 2, 1986**, while he was president of the Texas Research League. The Abilene Christian University Library preserved the recording, and the UNT-hosted Portal to Texas History identifies it as a primary source. ([The Portal to Texas History][25])

That video is arguably one of the most important discoveries from this research because it preserves Jared himself—not merely a list of accomplishments.

# *Managing Macroeconomic Policy: The Johnson Presidency*

During this period Jared also completed one of his major scholarly works.

With political scientist James E. Anderson, he wrote ***Managing Macroeconomic Policy: The Johnson Presidency***, published by the University of Texas Press in 1986 as part of the Administrative History of the Johnson Presidency series. ([JSTOR][26])

The book was not primarily a judgment about whether particular economic policies were correct. Instead, it examined **how presidential economic policy was managed**: information systems, policy coordination, coalition building, fiscal policy, monetary policy, wage-price policy, balance-of-payments issues, the Treasury, the Federal Reserve, the Council of Economic Advisers, and the Bureau of the Budget. The research drew on presidential documents and interviews with Johnson administration officials. ([JSTOR][26])

It is an especially fitting work in the context of Jared's career. Rather than viewing economics solely as equations or forecasts, the book examined how institutions actually organize and implement economic decisions.

---

# Mesa Petroleum, 1987–1989

In **1987**, Jared moved from public-policy research into the private energy sector.

TexEcon records him as **Vice President for Economics at Mesa Limited Partnership from 1987 through 1989**. UNT's later biography independently confirms that he held the vice-president-for-economics position at Amarillo-based Mesa, which it described at the time as the country's largest independent oil and gas company. ([TexEcon][1])

This was not as dramatic a departure from his earlier career as it might initially appear. Energy, mineral resources, petroleum markets, and the relationship between resource industries and public policy had been central to his scholarship since his doctoral work.

Mesa put those interests inside the operations of a major energy company.

---

# Texas A&M University, 1989–1999

In **1989**, Jared returned to academia at Texas A&M University's business school.

The most reliable chronology comes from UNT's 1999 announcement recruiting him away from A&M. It states that he had served as **Director of the Center for Business and Economic Analysis in the Lowry Mays College and Graduate School of Business since 1989**. It separately states that he had been a **Professor of Finance since 1992**, teaching corporate finance and money and capital markets. ([UNT Digital Library][3])

That distinction corrects the compressed chronology on TexEcon, which can be read as though both roles began simultaneously in 1989.

The safer timeline is therefore:

**1989–1999 — Director, Center for Business and Economic Analysis**
**1992–1999 — Professor of Finance**

At A&M, Jared again occupied the space between university scholarship and applied economic analysis.

---

# Education policy and year-round schooling

One example was his 1992 work on school calendars.

Jared was the lead author of the Texas A&M study ***Cost Effectiveness of Alternative Year Schooling***, an 83-page report examining alternative forms of year-round education. The study addressed costs, academic achievement, retention of learning, socialization, employment considerations, legal requirements, staffing, and effects on communities. ([ERIC][27])

The report continued to be cited in later scholarship about year-round education and school-calendar reform. ([JSTOR][28])

Again, the research theme is recognizable: rather than treating an educational proposal as an abstract idea, it asked what implementation would cost and what measurable effects it might have.

---

# NAFTA, energy, and regional economics

During the early 1990s, Jared also analyzed economic integration between Texas, the United States, and Mexico.

Contemporary reporting in 1993 records his analysis of NAFTA's potential implications for energy equipment, electricity generation, and exports to Mexico. ([Texas Tech University Newspapers][29])

By the mid-1990s he was frequently quoted as a regional economist. A 1996 *D Magazine* article, for example, identified him as director of Texas A&M's Center for Business and Economic Analysis and quoted his assessment of the Dallas and Texas consumer economy. ([D Magazine][30])

This public-facing role—explaining economic conditions to policymakers, businesses, journalists, and the public—would continue in his later consulting work.

---

# Adviser on Texas tax policy

UNT's official biography records that Jared served as an **adviser on state tax policy to Texas Lieutenant Governor Bob Bullock and the Texas House Select Committee on Taxation**. ([UNT Digital Library][3])

Contemporary reporting provides additional context. During debates over restructuring Texas school finance and taxes, Jared was identified as Bullock's representative on a panel analyzing possible revenue alternatives. His role was analytical rather than electoral: examining fiscal structures and supplying economic expertise to state decision-makers. ([Texas Tech University Newspapers][31])

This continued the public-policy strand of his career that had begun at the LBJ School and Texas Research League.

---

# Dean of Business at the University of North Texas, 1999–2004

In 1999, Jared was selected in a national search to become dean of the **College of Business Administration at the University of North Texas**.

UNT's announcement stated that his appointment would be effective **June 1, 1999**. Jared said one of his goals was to put the college at the forefront of North Texas economic development. University leaders emphasized his combination of academic, governmental, and business experience and his ties to the business community. ([UNT Digital Library][3])

The university's own magazine subsequently confirmed that he began work June 1. ([UNT Digital Library][32])

His tenure as dean can also now be dated accurately. UNT announced that he would step down after the spring commencement ceremony on **May 8, 2004**, and return to teaching. ([UNT Digital Library][33])

This corrects another misleading compression in the TexEcon biography, which currently lists him as “Professor, finance, dean” from 1999 to 2007. He remained associated with UNT after 2004, but **the deanship itself was 1999–2004**. UNT's institutional history explicitly says that in 2004 he resigned as dean and returned to the faculty as Professor of Finance. ([UNT Digital Library][34])

During his deanship, UNT reported that the College of Business created **four new outreach centers**, added the Distinguished Executive Lecture, and established a student-managed investment fund. ([UNT Digital Library][33])

Those developments are consistent with the goal Jared stated when he arrived: deeper engagement between the business school and the economy surrounding it.

---

# Professor after the deanship

Stepping down as dean did not immediately end Jared's academic involvement.

UNT records identify him as Professor of Finance after 2004, and his academic participation extended well beyond that date. As late as **2010**, he appears on a UNT doctoral dissertation committee as a minor professor. ([UNT Digital Library][34])

This suggests that “retirement” was more gradual than a single date implies. Administration ended first; teaching, mentoring, economic analysis, and consulting continued.

---

# TexEcon and later economic work

TexEcon identifies Jared as a principal beginning in **2007**. ([TexEcon][1])

The firm became a vehicle for continuing the type of applied economic work that had characterized much of his career.

In 2009, for example, the Texas Land Title Association identified **Dr. Jared Hazleton as its economist** in proceedings before the Texas Department of Insurance. His testimony addressed the condition of the Texas economy and housing market during the aftermath of the national financial crisis. ([Texas Land Title Association][35])

His original interest in industry structure also remained relevant. Decades after the sulphur study, he continued applying economic analysis to markets, regulation, prices, and public policy.

# Climate change and the Texas economy

One of Jared's later scholarly contributions illustrates how long his interest in natural resources and environmental economics persisted.

The University of Texas Press volume ***The Impact of Global Warming on Texas*** includes a chapter titled **“Economy” by Jared Hazleton**. Other chapters examine climate science, water, biodiversity, agriculture, cities, emissions, and policy. ([University of Texas Press][36])

The subject connects surprisingly well with work he had done roughly four decades earlier on sulphur resources, pollution-control technology, coastal land-use management, and environmental policy.

What might initially look like unrelated subjects across his bibliography instead form a persistent thread: **how natural resources, environmental constraints, markets, and public policy interact over long periods of time.**

Taken as a whole, Jared's published work does not fit neatly inside one economics specialty.

His career ranged through industrial organization, banking, natural-resource economics, environmental economics, regional economics, international development, land reform, public finance, energy economics, education policy, presidential administration, business economics, and climate impacts.

His work repeatedly asked some variation of the same question:

**What happens when institutions have to make real choices under economic constraints?**

That appears to be the most coherent intellectual thread running through his published career.

# Civic leadership and service

The available biographical record also shows sustained involvement outside his formal employment.

TexEcon lists Jared as **President of the Arthritis Foundation in Austin, 1970–1972; Chairman of United Way Texas, 1984–1986; Director of the Bryan Rotary Club, 1997–1999; and president of the Southwestern Economics Association**. ([TexEcon][1])

Those dates currently rely primarily on the TexEcon biographical record and have not all been independently verified through the organizations' historical archives. United Way of Denton County does independently retain Jared among its historical volunteer leadership, identifying him as a retired University of North Texas leader. ([United Way of Denton County][37])

The family obituary also emphasizes his long involvement in Rotary and the United Way. ([Legacy.com][4])

This civic work complements the professional record. Much of his economics concerned institutions and communities, and he also participated directly in civic organizations intended to strengthen them.

---

# Family and personal life

The public record is necessarily richer about Jared's career than about the private man his family knew.

His published obituary identifies Elaine as his wife of **66 years** and describes their relationship as beginning while they were students at the University of Oklahoma. ([Legacy.com][4])

He was survived by Elaine; children Frances Hazleton, Alan Hazleton, and Mark Hazleton and their spouses; five grandchildren; his sister Susan Swingen; and extended family. ([Legacy.com][4])

Family material adds the dimensions that institutional archives cannot: his Christian faith, years teaching Sunday School, love of conversation and stories, golf, old movies, and sports, particularly the Texas Rangers, Dallas Cowboys, and Oklahoma Sooners.

Those details matter because the documentary record could otherwise create the impression that Jared moved through life as an accumulation of titles: economist, professor, associate dean, dean, president, vice president, director.

The personal record describes something different—a husband, father and grandfather whose curiosity extended well beyond his profession and whose family remained central throughout it.

Even the acknowledgment in his first major book supports that picture. Long before the later family recollections were written, he ended his academic acknowledgments by turning back to his wife and family. ([Amazon S3][5])

---

# Final years and death

Jared remained intellectually active long after the formal peak of his administrative career. TexEcon, consulting, public economic analysis, academic committee work, and later writing demonstrate that retirement did not end his interest in research or economic affairs. ([Texas Land Title Association][35])

Dr. Jared Earl Hazleton died at home on **September 3, 2026**, nine days before his 89th birthday. The funeral-home record and published obituary both give his dates as **September 12, 1937 – September 3, 2026**. ([Dignity Memorial][38])

His Celebration of Life was scheduled for September 19, 2026, at Celebration Community Church in Fort Worth. ([Legacy.com][4])

Family recollections add one final detail that feels especially characteristic: he died at approximately five in the afternoon with Elaine beside him, giving poignant resonance to a phrase he often enjoyed—“It's five o'clock somewhere.”

# Assessment of his professional legacy

The documentary record supports a more interesting description of Jared Hazleton than simply “economist and educator.”

He was an **applied institutional economist** in the broadest sense of that phrase.

His early research examined industries and resources. His Federal Reserve work examined banking systems. His environmental work examined competing land-use goals. His Middle Eastern work examined development and land reform. His LBJ School research examined public institutions. His Washington deanship involved preparing people to work in those institutions. The Texas Research League examined the finances of governments. Mesa placed him inside a major resource company. Texas A&M connected academic research with regional business and public decisions. UNT put him in charge of educating future business leaders. TexEcon allowed him to keep analyzing economic systems after formal administration ended.

Across all of those settings, the recurring subject was not merely money.

It was **decision-making**.

How do markets respond to scarcity?
How should governments analyze competing demands?
How do institutions respond to economic change?
How does public policy affect local communities?
How do businesses plan under uncertainty?
How do resource and environmental constraints change economic choices?

Those questions connect work that otherwise seems to range improbably from New England fisheries to Jordanian irrigation to Lyndon Johnson's White House to Texas school calendars.

The public record therefore supports the central idea that has been emerging in the family account of his life:

**Jared spent a lifetime trying to understand how things worked—and then applying what he learned to real problems affecting institutions, communities, and people.**

For a career spanning more than half a century, that may be the most accurate summary of all.
